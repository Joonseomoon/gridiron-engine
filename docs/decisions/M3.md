### Benchmark method

- Release build (`-O3 -DNDEBUG`): `cmake -S . -B build-release -DCMAKE_BUILD_TYPE=Release`. Unoptimized builds are
  about 5.7× slower and are never used for timing.
- Workload: `simulate BAL SEA --games 100000 --seed 1000`, output discarded.
- One warm-up run, then seven runs timed with `/usr/bin/time -p`; report the median `real` time and games/s
  (100,000 ÷ median).
- Profiles use `build-profile/` (Release plus `-g -fno-omit-frame-pointer`) and macOS `sample` on 1,000,000 games at one
  thread.

No `malloc`, `operator new`, `memcpy`, `memmove`, or `std::string` function appears: the team names ("BAL", "SEA") fit
in libc++'s inline small-string buffer, so copying a `GameState` allocates nothing, and the compiler inlines the copy
into `applyOutcome` and `runGame`.

**Long-name experiment (temporary, not committed).** Renaming BAL to a 31-character name, longer than the inline
buffer, makes every `GameState` copy allocate. Same games and seeds; one thread, 100,000 games, median of 7:
**1.63 s** (range 1.57–1.82) vs 1.22 s, 34% slower. In the profile, `malloc`, `free`, `memmove`, `memset` and
`std::string` assignment together take 24% of samples, which shows the whole state is copied on every play, not once
per game. Converted to time per 100,000 games, `applyOutcome`'s own share fell from about 0.21 s to 0.15 s once the
name copy moved out of it into library calls, so with short names the inline string copies cost roughly 0.05 s, or 4–5%
of the run. Removing the copies is therefore expected to save on the order of 5–10% with short names, and to stop long
names from costing a third.

Of the parts of the profile that can change without changing output, the per-play copy is the largest. The rest is
random-number work (`normal_distribution`, `log`, `mt19937` generation and seeding), which is either fixed by the
algorithm or changes results when altered.

### M3.1 A target: normal-distribution reuse (decided 2026-11-05)

- **Decision:** M3.1 A optimizes normal sampling instead of the per-play `GameState` copy. Each game creates one
  `std::normal_distribution` in `runGame` and reuses it for every yardage and play-time draw, passing the mean and
  standard deviation per call. The `GameState` copy moves to M3.2 B ("allocation or data access").
- **Reason:** normal sampling plus `log` is about 50% of run time, the largest hot spot, while the copy is about 5–10%.
  libc++ generates normal values in pairs (polar method) and keeps the second inside the distribution object; building a
  new object for every draw throws that value away, so the `log`, `sqrt` and uniform draws are paid twice per pair used.
- **Consequence:** this changes which random numbers each play uses, so the seed → game mapping changes. That is a
  deliberate exception to "without changing its public behavior" in ROADMAP 3.1. It requires a new `kModelVersion`, new
  reference hashes, and a statistical check instead of the hash check: before and after, at 100,000+ games, the win rate
  and mean margin must agree within their 95% CIs. The distribution must be created per game, never per thread or
  `static`, so that game i still depends only on seed i and results stay identical at every thread count.

## M3.1 Developer B: parallel batches (2026-11-01)

Implemented --threads T with default 1 and a maximum of the reported hardware
thread count (fallback 1). The flag requires --games. This choice is pending
Developer A review.

Each worker writes a separate contiguous range in a pre-sized results vector.
Game i retains seed batchSeed + i. All workers finish before aggregation.
CSV metadata records the requested thread count.

## M3.1 Developer A: normal-distribution reuse (2026-11-05)

Implemented as decided above: `RandomSource` (`src/engine/RandomSource.h`) holds a reference to the game's `mt19937`
and one `std::normal_distribution<double>`; `runGame` creates it once per game, and every sampler takes it in place of
the rng, passing `{mean, sd}` on each normal draw.

**Timing** (Developer A machine, `e4dfb8a`, one thread, 1,000,000 games, 1 warm-up, median of 7):

| Revision | Median s | Range s | Games/s | vs before |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Before (`1595012`) | 12.30 | 12.23–12.57 | 81,301 | 1.00× |
| After (`e4dfb8a`) | 9.12 | 9.09–9.24 | 109,649 | 1.35× (26% less time) |

The estimate was 20–25% from the profile (normal sampling about 50%, half of its work saved), so the result is at the
top of that range.

**Output:** the terminal hash for `simulate BAL SEA --games 10000 --seed 1000` changes to
`f42d5118fcc886188cf1ec84a164fd52f6324211` and is identical at 1 and 4 threads. All 206 tests pass with only the
mechanical signature change in the sampler and integration tests; none pinned exact seeded values.

**Model version:** `kModelVersion` is now `m3-v1` (was `m1-v1`). Rule: bump it whenever the same seed and teams can
give a different game, whether from sampling, ratings, constants, or rules; name it `m<milestone>-v<n>`, counting up
within a milestone. Output-only changes, such as formatting or new summary fields, don't bump it. The CSV tests spell
the version out rather than reading `kModelVersion`, so every bump has to update them deliberately.

**Statistical check** (BAL SEA, 1,000,000 games per run, before = `39a8056`, after = `e4dfb8a`). Exact counts:

| Seed | Revision | BAL wins | SEA wins | Ties | BAL win rate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1000 | Before | 211,389 | 762,652 | 25,959 | 0.21139 |
| 1000 | After | 210,944 | 762,928 | 26,128 | 0.21094 |
| 5000000 | Before | 210,526 | 763,423 | 26,051 | 0.21053 |
| 5000000 | After | 209,914 | 763,886 | 26,200 | 0.20991 |

Pooled over both seeds (2,000,000 games each), the win rate differs by 0.00053 (z = 1.3) and the tie rate by 0.00016
(z = 1.0), both within sampling noise. Means and sds agree to the printed two decimals or within 0.01–0.02. Seed 1000
showed total sd 9.15 vs 9.17 (z between 1 and 3 given the rounding); with seed 5000000 both are 9.18, so it was noise.
The distribution is unchanged, as expected: the two values of a polar-method pair are independent standard normals.
